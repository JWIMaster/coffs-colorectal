## Definition

The rectum is the last part (approximately 12cm) of the large bowel before the anus. A cancer is a malignant growth of tissue. Malignant means that has the potential to spread to other areas of the body. It does NOT mean that it has already spread. The term “bowel cancer” refers to cancer of the colon or the rectum. Despite many similarities between the rectum and the colon there are significant differences in the way cancer is treated between these two areas. It is therefore important to clarify where the cancer is exactly.

## Diagnosis

Rectal cancer can often be diagnosed by performing a rectal examination. A colonoscopy and biopsy (removal of a very small part of the tumour for testing) will usually be required to confirm the diagnosis and further assess the cancer.

Screening tests, such as the bowel scan, detect microscopic amounts of blood in the faeces. They do not detect cancer. Many cancers bleed a small amount into the bowel but there are many other causes for a positive screening test apart from cancer.

Following diagnosis further tests are usually performed to determine the stage of the cancer. The most important aspect of this is determining the size of the rectal cancer and detecting any spread through the wall of the bowel. The best test to assess this is an MRI scan. Unfortunately Medicare does not cover the cost of this MRI scan despite it being the recommended by the National Health and Medical Research Council.

A CT scan of the chest and abdomen are also used to detect evidence of spread to other organs.

## Treatment

The main treatment for rectal cancer is surgery to remove the entire and the lymph glands around that area. In most cases the rectum can be removed and the bowel rejoined to avoid a permanent stoma (bag). A temporary stoma will usually be used to “rest” the join and allow it to heal safely. A second, smaller, operation will be required to remove the bag. In some cases, depending on the exact location of the cancer and other technical factors, it may not be possible to rejoin the bowel. In this case the anus is usually removed as well and a permanent bag will be required.

The other major treatments for rectal cancer are radiation therapy and chemotherapy. Many people are advised to have treatment with chemotherapy and radiotherapy before surgery. This advice is largely based on the results of the MRI scan.

## Follow up

There are several reasons why your surgeon will continue to see you after surgery. Initially it is important to ensure that the recovery proceeds well. In the longer term it is important to be monitored for recurrence of the cancer. This will involve regular blood tests and visits for 5 years after the time of surgery. Recurrence of a cancer may still be curable if it is detected early.

In addition there is an ongoing risk of developing more polyps in the bowel or even a second bowel cancer. Colonoscopy will be recommended periodically depending on the nature of the initial cancer.

The diagnosis of cancer and the treatment that it requires are often devestating to an individual and their family. There are many supports available to help during this difficult period and afterwards. Speak to your surgeon or general practitioner for help.
