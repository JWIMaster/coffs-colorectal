Surgery for rectal prolapse

Rectal prolapse is when the rectum drops down and comes out through the anus. It does not turn into cancer, but it usually needs an operation to repair it.

Key facts

  • Full-thickness prolapse needs surgery
  • Different from haemorrhoids
  • Abdominal repair, often keyhole
  • Perineal repair for frailer patients
  • Function may take up to a year to settle

What rectal prolapse is

Rectal prolapse happens when the rectum, the last part of the large bowel, loses its normal attachments inside the pelvis and telescopes out through the anus, turning inside out. This is called full-thickness prolapse.

A milder form is mucosal prolapse, where only the inner lining of the bowel comes out. This is different from full-thickness prolapse and is treated differently.

Rectal prolapse is not common. It affects mostly adults, and women aged 50 and over are several times more likely than men to develop it. It is linked with long-standing straining and constipation, and with childbirth, although many people who develop it have never had children.

It is not the same as haemorrhoids. Haemorrhoids involve only the inner lining near the anal opening, while rectal prolapse involves a full segment of bowel from higher up.

Symptoms

  • Mucus or fluid discharge and soiling of underwear.
  • Bleeding from the protruding tissue.
  • Constipation, which the prolapse itself can worsen by blocking the passage of stool.
  • Difficulty controlling wind or stool, because the stretched sphincter muscles work less well.
  • A dragging or bulging sensation in the pelvis.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis is usually made by examining the anus and rectum. If the prolapse is not visible in the rooms, your surgeon may ask you to strain while sitting on a toilet, or to bring a photograph taken at home. Seeing the prolapsed tissue is important to tell it apart from haemorrhoids, because the treatment is very different.

You may have tests such as an ultrasound or manometry to check the sphincter muscles, a test to see how quickly stool moves through the bowel, or a colonoscopy to check the rest of the bowel. These help your surgeon choose the right operation.

Perineal and abdominal approaches

There are two main ways to repair a rectal prolapse: through the abdomen or from below, through the perineum, which is the area between the buttocks.

Perineal operations include the Altemeier procedure, where the prolapsed bowel is removed and the remaining bowel is reconnected to the anus, and the Delorme procedure, where the inner lining of the prolapsed rectum is stripped away and the muscle wall is folded and stitched to shorten it.

Abdominal operations include rectopexy, where the rectum is freed, lifted back into place and secured to the back wall of the pelvis, sometimes with mesh to reinforce it. If constipation has been a long-standing problem, part of the colon may also be removed at the same time. Abdominal repair may be done by open surgery or by keyhole surgery.

Sometimes a prolapse is hidden inside the body and does not come out. This is called internal intussusception, and it is more difficult to diagnose.

Why a surgeon chooses one approach

The abdominal approach generally gives better long-term control of the prolapse, with a lower chance of it coming back, and it tends to give better results for bowel function. It is a bigger operation and is usually offered to people who are medically fit enough for it.

The perineal approach is often chosen for older or frailer people, or those with other serious medical conditions that make a bigger operation risky. It can be done with less anaesthetic, usually causes less pain, and often means a shorter stay in hospital. The trade-off is a higher chance that the prolapse will return.

Your surgeon will consider your age, your general health, the size of the prolapse, your bowel habit and your continence when recommending an approach. It is reasonable to ask why one approach is being recommended over the other.

Bowel function after repair

Many people find their symptoms improve considerably after a successful repair. Constipation often improves, and continence can improve as the stretched sphincter muscles recover.

It is also honest to say that function does not always improve. In a small number of people, constipation can be worse after an abdominal rectopexy, and some people develop it for the first time. Others may notice changes in sexual function after extensive pelvic surgery.

Bowel function can continue to change for up to a year after the operation, so it can take time to know the final result. Diet, fibre, fluids and sometimes stool softeners or laxatives help. Your surgeon may also discuss pelvic floor exercises or other treatments.

Recovery

Recovery depends on the approach. After a perineal operation, many people are home within a day or two and are comfortable quite quickly. After an abdominal operation, you will usually be in hospital for several days and will need several weeks before returning to full activity.

Avoid heavy lifting and straining for about six weeks or as advised. Straining after the repair should be avoided, because it can put the repair at risk. Keep your stools soft with fibre, fluids and stool softeners if needed.

The prolapse can come back. The risk is higher after perineal procedures than after abdominal ones. Continuing to avoid straining and treating constipation helps protect the repair.

Risks and complications

Every operation carries some risk. The risks below are the ones worth knowing about before you decide. Rare complications are still possible, and Dr Sutherland will discuss how they apply to you.

  • The prolapse returning

    Recurrence is the main long-term risk. It is more common after perineal procedures than after abdominal repair. Avoiding straining and treating constipation helps reduce the risk.

  • Bleeding

    Bleeding can occur during or after the operation, and may need a transfusion or further treatment.

  • Infection

    Infection can affect the wound or the pelvis, particularly if mesh is used. It is usually treated with antibiotics and sometimes drainage.

  • Leak from the reconnected bowelRare but serious

    After an Altemeier procedure, where the bowel is rejoined to the anus, the join can leak. This is uncommon but serious and may need further surgery. It is more likely in frail patients.

  • Narrowing of the anal opening

    Scarring after a perineal procedure can narrow the anal opening, making it harder to pass stool. This may need stretching or further treatment.

  • Constipation may worsen or appear

    Some people develop new or worse constipation after an abdominal rectopexy. Fibre, fluids and stool softeners may be needed. Discuss this with your surgeon if it happens.

  • Changes in sexual functionRare but serious

    Extensive dissection in the pelvis can affect sexual function in some people. Your surgeon will discuss this risk with you before the operation.

  • Complications of a major operationRare but serious

    As with any major surgery, there are risks of blood clots in the legs or lungs, heart and lung complications, and, rarely, death. These are considered carefully when choosing the right operation for you.

Questions people ask

Is rectal prolapse the same as haemorrhoids?
No. Haemorrhoids involve the inner lining near the anal opening. Rectal prolapse involves a full segment of bowel from higher up. They look similar to some people but are treated very differently.
Do I have to have surgery?
Surgery is the only treatment that repairs the prolapse. It does not turn into cancer, but the prolapse tends to get worse over time and the risk of continence problems increases, so repair is usually recommended.
Why am I being offered a perineal operation rather than an abdominal one?
The abdominal approach generally gives better long-term results, but it is a bigger operation. It is usually offered to people fit enough for it. The perineal approach is often safer for older or frailer people, with a higher chance of the prolapse returning.
Will my constipation and continence improve?
They often do improve after a successful repair, but this is not guaranteed. Some people find constipation gets worse, and bowel function can take up to a year to settle.
How long is the recovery?
After a perineal operation, many people are home within a day or two. After an abdominal operation, expect several days in hospital and several weeks before returning to full activity. Avoid heavy lifting for about six weeks.
Can it come back?
It can. Recurrence is more common after perineal procedures. Avoiding straining and keeping your stools soft helps protect the repair.
